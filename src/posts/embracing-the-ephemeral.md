# Embracing the Ephemeral: How to Get the Most out of a Hackathon


> **No generative AI was used to write this post.**


I remember having a coffee on a chilly Monday morning with my boss during my first internship. I needed the coffee especially that morning, since I'd been experiencing jetlag as the result of a flight back from a hackathon the night prior. Since I'd taken an unpaid day off on Friday to fly out, my boss was interested to know what I'd been up to instead of maximizing shareholder value at work. At one point during my recounting of the weekend's events, he stopped me and asked, "why?" He wanted to know why I kept going to hackathons. He wanted to know how I could be satisfied with continuing to cycle through projects, never getting extended time to build something seriously before moving on to something else. He wanted to know why I put myself through grueling weekends again and again. And to be honest, I wanted to know too. I didn't have a concrete reason, I just knew that whenever I saw a hackathon coming up, I felt a compulsion to register. I don't have all the answers now, but getting to experience hackathons as a hacker, mentor, and organizer, have opened my eyes on how to truly embrace those ephemeral weekends.


My first hackathon was a local competition in 2022 called Hack the Change that I took part in on the urging of a friend. I'd love to say that we won the competition, and that's what sparked the flame that would lead me to compete obsessively in every hackathon I could find for the next few years. But we didn't win. Perhaps then I'd love to say that despite not winning, I was proud of what my team and I developed at our first hackathon and at least I got to apply my skills to make something cool. But [the project](https://youtu.be/34M_vPXtoVU) genuinely **sucked**. Perhaps then, I'd love to say that presenting the project was a great experience to get feedback and hopefully come back stronger next time. But we didn't even submit - so there was no judging or feedback. It seemed that everything that probably could go wrong, did go wrong.


The first fault came a few hours into our planning - we split up and decided to work on different parts of the application totally separate, and left about an hour on the second day to integrate. Two of our team members ran into car troubles on their way on day two, so they were relegated to hearing about the project's meltdown over text messages. In the end, we couldn't find a way to integrate the backend (a literal Jupyter notebook) with any sort of frontend. By the time we gave up and decided to just submit it, we found out our submission required a YouTube video demo, which we couldn't get uploaded in time.


There was no prize, no feedback, no new connections made, and from the perspective of the hackathon's submission system - no project at all. I was a second year software engineering student, and I was embarrassed that the best I could do was an unsubmitted Jupyter notebook that was effectively just a Google search wrapper. My motivation to remake the project completely died when, a few months later, this thing called ChatGPT became all the rage, and replaced the need for that project idea entirely.


I started with the story of my first hackathon because it tells a tale of someone who got almost nothing out of a hackathon. I didn't get to learn new skills, I didn't meet anyone interesting, and I didn't get any formalized feedback on how I could improve. All of this motivated me to try to redeem myself at another hackathon.


But back in 2022, it was a lot harder to find hackathons than it is now, and it was even harder to convince my friends with technical experience to compete in them with me. I'd have to wait another four months before my next hackathon - a project where my team made a simple [project improving emergency room wait times](https://www.linkedin.com/posts/zaid-ahmed-swe_team-hackathon-medical-activity-7032499517455290368-RFUv?utm_source=share&utm_medium=member_desktop&rcm=ACoAADjnN3sB4j_wI-A8CirO1PXiijedHBLVMHQ) that would achieve third place. That would kick off a win streak of three hackathons, followed by a losing streak of far more, some more wins, co-founding Calgary's largest 36-hour hackathon, winning Calgary's biggest hackathon, being named in [MLH’s Top 50 Hackers of 2026](https://top.mlh.com/2026/profiles/zaid-ahmed), and finally, this blog post.


Through each win or loss, I learned something about hackathons, and more importantly, myself. As a developer, engineer, entrepreneur, and advocate. This post is not about how to win hackathons - to be honest, winning was not _always_ my chief goal. You can still make the most out of a hackathon without going home with a prize, and I'll be detailing my opinion on how to achieve that in the rest of this post.


### Defining Individual Goals


There are lots of reasons for attending a hackathon, and it's important to decide why you're going - whether you want gold and glory or just some free food, it's important to define a high-level goal. The best hackathons will have a lot going on: companies to network with, people to meet, projects to work on, and maybe even llamas to pet and Squid Game challenges to play (TreeHacks 2025 had a lot of stress-busters).

![A llama at a hackathon stress-relief area](/llama.jpg "Never thought my first time seeing llamas would be at a hackathon.")

It's tempting, but you simply cannot have it all. If you want to win at a competitive hackathon, you'll need to give most of your time to the actual project itself, which may not enable a ton of fun side-encounters. If you want to network with people at the career booths, you'll likely have to sacrifice some time working on your project. Of course, you can balance different priorities over the course of the weekend, but you're likely to leave the hackathon unsatisfied if you wanted to have the most fun, the biggest prize, and the most LinkedIn connections managed.


It's worth mentioning that I am familiar with the all-nighter approach to a hackathon - maximizing fun and networking during the scheduled daily hours, and then working through the night. It might be just because I'm getting older now, but I can't recommend this. There _is_ such a thing as being too tired to think. When the project breaks and needs a hotfix, the well-slept hacker is less likely to snap. And the next day, when judges come around, the lively presenter is likely to take home the prize.


### Finding a Team


The ideal composition of your team depends on whatever you defined your goal as. Sometimes it can be tempting to just try to join up with the most "cracked" people, but I'd argue it's far more important to find people who share the same goal. I've made this mistake before. Once, I teamed up with people who had won the same hackathon the prior year. They were incredibly talented and enjoyable to work with; but I found that there was a disconnect in priorities. Since they had already won, they didn't have the same hunger as I did and decided that this hackathon was more of an opportunity to network and collect free swag from company booths. And there's nothing wrong with that - those are valid reasons to come to hackathons. The mistake was my own for not having an honest conversation with my prospective teammates.


So then how should you construct a team that maximizes your chances of winning?


#### Be Multidisciplinary


It's underrated how multi-faceted a truly excellent hackathon submission is. The best projects often have excellent technical implementations, yes, but they also often have intuitive design and seamless project management behind the scenes. Most hackathons have competitors innovating on fields that can range from healthcare and education to finance and security. To develop effective solutions that address these problems, you either need to spend several hours doing research into those fields and learning about the problems in them, or have someone on your team who already has that requisite knowledge.


#### Find Builders Who Solve Problems


Creating applications has become cheaper than ever; in terms of cost, time, and effort. Overall, this is a great development for the average person, as it means that there are fewer barriers for someone to finally develop that million-dollar idea they've always had. Unfortunately, this also means that there's a lot more building without understanding. Much of the discourse about the tech industry online motivates people to build and ship fast to stand out. I agree with this, but with a caveat; what they're building should actually solve a problem. Look to teams with people who solved specific, niche problems important to them rather than someone who built yet another social networking/budgeting/dating/AI application with no additional value over mainstream solutions.


### Meeting People


I've met some incredible people over the years at hackathons. Naturally, you'll get to know your own teammates quite well over the course of the hack, but I try to make a point to also talk to as many people as possible who aren't on my team. I understand that it can be daunting to speak to strangers, especially knowing that everyone around you is a direct competitor. There are ways to minimize this fear, however.


Hackathons can be stressful, so it’s best to talk to someone when they’re not doing something urgent. For instance, having a casual chat with someone during the final hour when everyone is scrambling to finish is probably not the best first introduction.


The best time to talk to someone is when it seems like they’re not working on their project.. There are some places that are more ideal for initiating conversation, like activities such as video games or sports. And when it's time for a meal, a slow-moving food line also makes for a great place to mingle.


People representing sponsor companies are almost always open to chat. Connecting with hackathon sponsors is a great way to learn more about different companies, and the paths available to you in the tech industry. Especially if you’re building with their technology (API, SDK) it’s great to bounce your ideas off of sponsors as well.


### Judging


Even if you aren't going for a prize, I highly recommend submitting a project just for the experience of judging. Having a project judged at a hackathon is an incredibly formative experience: you have to condense 36 hours of problem identification, research, planning, and development all into a 3-4 minute pitch. Judging allows you to practice relevant skills you’ll need at many points in life, and you won’t often get the opportunity to receive such candid feedback.


#### Understanding > Impression


It's true that you need to impress the judges if you want to get a high score. However, regardless of the outcome, you'll come away with a lot more by making sure the judges actually understand everything you did during the hackathon. They should understand that you did research and identified a real problem. They should understand that you ran into issues that you didn't foresee. They should understand how much you learned and grew during the competition. All of this establishes rapport with the judge, and so when you demo your solution to them they know the full story. High-level diagrams explaining your architecture and design go a long way in helping them understand technically what's going on - but only once you've laid the groundwork of why this project solves the issue. When judges understand everything that went into the project, there's a high chance they'll be impressed.


### Self-Reflection


#### When to Reflect


Especially after losses, it's necessary to analyze what happened to come up with better strategies for next time. However, the loss needs to marinate a bit. If you start trying to break things down too early, you're too close to the problem. When you can only see a zoomed-in section of it, your analysis will be inherently incomplete. Consider taking some time away from thinking about the hackathon so that you can return to the issue with a fresh mind.


#### How to Reflect


After a hackathon, it can be helpful  to come up with two or three concrete behaviours your team did well, and two or three concrete behaviours that your team should have done. It’s important to make sure your goals are actionable. Broad claims like "we should have done more research on the problem" are very difficult to actually translate into your action items for your next hackathon. Instead, you could phrase the earlier claim as "we won't write any code until we have clearly identified a specific problem that our solution addresses."


Judging feedback is also essential for reflection. The more you try to understand the judges' point of view, the better chance you’ll have at knowing what they might be looking for at the next hackathon.


Finally, it's never a bad idea to look at the winning projects. What's more important than the actual technical solution, however, is to understand the differences in their project structure and framing of the problem, and how they were able to get the judges to understand and appreciate their solution.


### Conclusion


Hackathons have given me so much. As a competitor, they taught me how to build iteratively and quickly, how to forge lasting bonds with people I just met, and how to pick myself up after continued losses. As a founder, they gave me the enjoyment of watching people find their place in the tech community. And so, even if that feeling lasts only for an ephemeral weekend, I think it's worth it to continue chasing every one of those moments. And I hope that by reading this post, you might have a bit better of an idea of how to appreciate those moments yourself.


Happy Hacking >;)
